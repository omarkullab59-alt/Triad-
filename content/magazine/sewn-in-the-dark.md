The railway arch shakes every four minutes. Kofi Adair no longer notices. He is cutting the sleeve of a 1970s field jacket into a lapel, and the train passing overhead is just another rhythm in a room full of them.

## Surplus as source

Adair works almost exclusively with deadstock military garments: parkas, liners, trousers, the canvas covers of things nobody remembers. He unpicks them completely, irons the pieces flat and reads them like a map. Where the fabric is faded tells him where a body once bent.

> I am not upcycling. I am translating. The jacket already said something. I am making it say it in a different language.

His pieces are sold by appointment, mostly to people who found him through other people. There is no website. There is a phone number that sometimes works.

![Pattern pieces pinned to a wall](https://picsum.photos/seed/triad-sewn-fig1/1800/1200?grayscale "The wall of pieces. Nothing is thrown away.")

## Refusing the runway

Asked why he has never staged a show, Adair laughs. A runway, he says, asks clothes to perform for twelve minutes. He would rather they performed for twelve years.

That patience is visible in the work. Seams are reinforced three times. Pockets are placed where hands actually go. The garments look severe on a hanger and change completely on a body — they soften, they settle, they start to belong to someone.

---

He is currently working on fourteen pieces. He does not know when they will be finished. "When they stop arguing with me," he says.
